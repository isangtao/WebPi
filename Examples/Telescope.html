01 · The Families

Three ways to bend starlight

Every consumer telescope is built around one of three optical strategies — lenses, mirrors, or a combination of both. Five designs dominate the market, each trading off light-gathering, portability, and upkeep in its own signature way.

Refracting · lenses Reflecting · mirrors Catadioptric · lenses + mirrors
Light path · Refractor

Refractor

Refracting

The classic all-lens design

Light passes through a corrected objective lens at the front of the tube and is focused at the rear, where the eyepiece sits. The all-glass tube stays sealed for life — the original “point and go” telescope.

Strengths

  • Zero maintenance — nothing to collimate
  • Sharp, high-contrast Moon & planet views
  • Compact, travel-friendly, sturdy
  • Sealed tube keeps images steady

Watch-outs

  • Color fringing in cheaper achromats
  • Priciest per inch of aperture
  • Small apertures limit deep-sky detail
  • Best forMoon, planets, wide-field
  • Entry pricefrom ~$200
  • Typical aperture60 – 120 mm
Light path · Newtonian

Newtonian Reflector

Reflecting

The deep-sky workhorse

A concave primary mirror at the bottom of the tube reflects light back to a flat 45° secondary, which diverts it out the side to the eyepiece. Mirrors are cheap to make large — which is why every serious deep-sky scope uses one.

Strengths

  • Huge apertures for the money
  • No chromatic aberration — ever
  • Easy to upgrade and customize
  • Open tube = fast image stability

Watch-outs

  • Needs occasional collimation
  • Tubes are bulky and less portable
  • Secondary spider blocks some light
  • Best forDeep sky, budget builders
  • Entry pricefrom ~$250
  • Typical aperture130 – 250 mm
Light path · Schmidt-Cassegrain

Schmidt-Cassegrain

Catadioptric

The versatile travel all-rounder

A Schmidt corrector plate feeds light to a curved primary mirror at the rear, which reflects it through a hole in its center to a back-mounted eyepiece — folding a ~2,000 mm focal length into a 40 cm tube.

Strengths

  • Long focal length in a short tube
  • Sealed — almost no collimation
  • One scope for Moon, planets & deep sky
  • GoTo-ready in almost every setup

Watch-outs

  • Slow to cool — first 20 min are soft
  • Expensive per inch of aperture
  • Narrower field than a reflector
  • Best forMoon & planets, travel
  • Entry pricefrom ~$600 (8")
  • Typical aperture203 – 254 mm
Light path · Maksutov-Cassegrain

Maksutov-Cassegrain

Catadioptric

The sharp, sealed compact

A thick meniscus corrector lens — whose back surface also acts as the tiny secondary mirror — works with a spherical primary to deliver a very long, very sharp f/12 image through the rear of the tube.

Strengths

  • Superb contrast for planetary work
  • Fully sealed — no dust, no collimation
  • Compact, sturdy, cheaper than SCTs
  • Holds alignment year after year

Watch-outs

  • Very long focal length — low max power
  • Narrow field of view
  • Slower to cool than an open tube
  • Best forPlanetary purists
  • Entry pricefrom ~$400 (5")
  • Typical aperture127 – 150 mm
Light path · Dobsonian (open truss)

Dobsonian

Reflecting

The giant-slayer on a wooden box

A Newtonian tube riding in an alt-azimuth rocker base — two pivots, one knob each. It drops the counterweights and gears, so the money you save goes straight into aperture. The favorite of backyard astronomers since the 1970s.

Strengths

  • Biggest aperture per dollar in consumer optics
  • Rock-solid, dead-simple platform
  • No counterweights, no belts, no gears
  • Scales from 8" up to 25"+ with ease

Watch-outs

  • Manual pointing — or add a GoTo kit
  • Heavy and awkward to transport
  • Base takes up real floor space
  • Best forGalaxies, nebulae, cluster-hopping
  • Entry pricefrom ~$400 (8")
  • Typical aperture200 – 635 mm

03 · Choosing Wisely

Five decisions that matter more than the box says

Marketing copy loves big magnification numbers. Here are the criteria that actually determine whether a telescope earns a permanent spot in your night routine.

01

Aperture first

The single most important spec. Aperture is the diameter of the primary lens or mirror, and it sets how much light and how much detail you get. Never buy “magnification” — it's just aperture with eyepieces swapped in.

02

The mount is half the telescope

A great optic on a wobbly mount is a toy. Alt-azimuth mounts are intuitive and GoTo-friendly; equatorial mounts track with one motor and enable long-exposure astrophotography. Budget for a sturdy mount before extra features.

03

Optics versus upkeep

Sealed refractors and catadioptrics are set-and-forget. Reflectors deliver the most light per dollar but need collimation every few months. Pick the maintenance level you're genuinely willing to perform.

04

Be honest about portability

If it doesn't fit in your car and on your shoulder, it won't travel. A smaller scope used weekly teaches you more than a larger one that stays covered in the garage. Travel size: under ~4 kg, under 70 cm.

05

Budget for the whole kit, not just the tube

A decent finderscope, a spare eyepiece, a red-light flashlight, a sturdy table, and dew-strap protection can run 10–20% of the scope's price. The first eyepiece included with a kit is rarely the one you'll use most — a good 25 mm and a 10 mm will cover most nights.

Rule of thumb

Start somewhere between 4" and 8" of aperture. An 8" Dobsonian gathers roughly six times the light of a 4" refractor — and often costs less. Buy the biggest aperture you can comfortably carry and point, then upgrade eyepieces and the mount as your interest (and your eyes) adapt.

05 · Speak the Language

A pocket glossary

The eight terms that cover 90% of every spec sheet and forum thread.

Aperture
The diameter of the main lens or mirror. The #1 spec — more aperture means more light and finer detail.
Focal length
The distance over which light is brought to focus. Combined with aperture, it sets your magnification range and field of view.
f-ratio
Focal length ÷ aperture. “Fast” (f/4) scopes give wide fields at low power; “slow” (f/10+) give high magnification reach.
Collimation
Keeping all optical surfaces precisely aligned. Reflectors need it occasionally; refractors and SCTs rarely do.
Alt-azimuth
A two-axis mount: up/down and left/right. Intuitive to use, the default for Dobsonians and most GoTo mounts.
Equatorial
A two-axis mount aligned to the sky's rotation. Tracks objects with a single motor and enables long-exposure astrophotography.
GoTo
A computerized mount that slews to any named object — planets, nebulae, comets — at the touch of a button.
Eyepiece
The small lens you look through. Magnification = focal length of the scope ÷ focal length of the eyepiece. Cheap to upgrade.
