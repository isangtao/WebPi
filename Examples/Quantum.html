Blackbody Radiation & the Birth of the Quantum

Every object glows. The color and brightness of that glow obeyed no known law — until a clerk at the Berlin Patent Office made a desperate, brilliant guess.

The puzzle

A hollow cavity heated to temperature T emits "blackbody" radiation through a tiny hole. Classical physics — the Rayleigh–Jeans law — says each electromagnetic mode should carry kT of energy, and the modes pile up without bound toward the ultraviolet. The prediction: an infinitely hot sky. This was the ultraviolet catastrophe.

What was observed

The real spectrum climbs, peaks, and falls off. Planck's fit worked only if the cavity's oscillators could exchange energy in discrete packets E = nhν. He called it "an act of desperation". It was the birth of the quantum.

Why it matters

A single constant, h = 6.626×10⁻³⁴ J·s, entered physics. Raise the temperature and the peak slides exactly as Wien's law demands; the dashed classical curve meanwhile races off toward the ultraviolet — the catastrophe, on display.

The Photoelectric Effect & the Photon

Shine light on a clean metal plate and electrons pop off. The classical wave story made one prediction that simply did not happen.

The puzzle

If light is a wave, its energy comes from intensity: dim light should eventually eject electrons and no threshold should exist. But below a cutoff frequency ν₀, no light of any brightness frees an electron — and above it, electrons leave instantly with energies that are independent of brightness.

What was observed

Einstein's 1905 move: light arrives in quanta — photons — each carrying energy hν. A photon gives its whole energy to a single electron: Kmax = hν − φ. Intensity changes only how many photons arrive, so it scales the current, never the energy per electron.

Why it matters

The photon was real. This effect runs every photodiode, solar cell and light sensor you own. Push the frequency below the red tick (ν₀) and the current dies — crank the intensity to maximum and it stays dead. That single fact killed wave-only light.

Compton Scattering: Photons With Momentum

If photons carry energy, they must carry momentum too. Compton found a collision that proved it — head-on, in plain sight.

The puzzle

X-rays scatter off the loose electrons in graphite. A classical wave should emerge with essentially the same wavelength. Yet the scattered X-rays were measurably longer, and the shift depended only on the scattering angle — no classical model predicted such a clean relation.

What was observed

The shift obeys Δλ = (h/mc)(1 − cosθ) — exactly the bookkeeping of a particle collision: a photon with momentum h/λ trades energy and momentum with a free electron. Rotate the detector and the shift follows the formula to four significant figures.

Why it matters

Compton's 1923 result settled the debate: light is quantized in its momentum, not just its energy. It underpins X-ray and neutron diffraction, and it is the cleanest single demonstration that "particle" and "wave" are not mutually exclusive.

Stern–Gerlach: The Beam That Split in Two

Point a beam of silver atoms through an uneven magnetic field. Classical physics predicts a smear. Nature writes two dots.

The puzzle

Silver atoms carry one unpaired electron and thus a magnetic moment. Classically that moment can point in any direction, so passing through a gradient field should fan the beam into a continuous band on the detector screen.

What was observed

In 1922 Stern and Gerlach saw exactly two spots: spin-up, spin-down. Angular momentum is quantized — measured along any axis it only ever returns ±ħ/2. Send the beam through a second analyzer rotated 90° and the two spots split again, 50/50, as if the first measurement were forgotten.

Why it matters

Spin-½ is the single most important two-level system in physics: the qubit, MRI, electron spin resonance, atomic clocks. Toggle the second analyzer below to see the surprise that keeps quantum information honest.

Electron Diffraction: Matter Waves Confirmed

Louis de Broglie bet the physics community that electrons have wavelengths. Two years later a lab accident in New Jersey proved him right.

The puzzle

de Broglie (1924): every particle with momentum p has a wavelength λ = h/p. An electron accelerated through voltage V has λ = h/√(2meV), so a beam of electrons should diffract off a crystal exactly like X-rays, satisfying Bragg's law nλ = 2d sinθ.

What was observed

Davisson and Germer fired electrons at a nickel target — an oxygen-baked accident had grown a giant single crystal. At just the right voltage a sharp peak appeared at the Bragg angle, and as the voltage rose the peak slid exactly where the matter-wave formula demanded. (G. P. Thomson got the same result the same year, through thin films.)

Why it matters

Matter is a wave. Electron microscopes, low-energy electron diffraction and half of condensed-matter physics rest on it. Sweep the voltage and watch the diffraction peak hunt its angle.

The Double Slit: Dots That Add to a Wave

The entire content of quantum mechanics in one image: dots, that add up to a wave.

The puzzle

Fire electrons, one at a time, through two slits. Each lands as a single dot — a particle. But the dots accumulate into bright and dark interference fringes — a wave. How does one dot "know" about the other slit?

What was observed

Tonomura's 1989 version made it vivid: the fringe pattern builds up dot by dot, from single-particle impacts. Now turn on a which-path detector: the fringes collapse into two plain bands, exactly classical. Measurement does not merely observe the quantum world — it reshapes it.

Why it matters

This is the measurement problem in its purest form, and the reason "wavefunction" and "collapse" are still debated a century later. Let the pattern build for a minute — it is the most beautiful image in physics.

The Hydrogen Spectrum: Orbits Become Levels

The simplest atom in the universe emits only certain colors of light. No one could explain why — until orbits became energy levels.

The puzzle

Hydrogen's spectrum is a set of razor-sharp lines (Balmer series: 410, 434, 486, 656 nm) that fit Balmer's 1885 formula with no theory behind it. Why those wavelengths and no others?

What was observed

Bohr's 1913 atom: electrons live only in quantized levels En = −13.6 eV/n². When an electron falls between levels it emits a photon of exactly that energy difference — every line in the spectrum is a transition. Excite the atom and watch the Balmer lines build on the right: the same pattern etched into every nebula photograph.

Why it matters

Bohr's circular orbits were soon replaced by Schrödinger's orbitals, but the level structure survived. Atomic clocks, lasers and all of spectroscopy run on quantized transitions like these.

Bell's Theorem: Spooky Action, Measured

Einstein called it "spooky action at a distance". Bell turned the complaint into an inequality — and experiments broke the inequality.

The puzzle

Two entangled photons fly to Alice and Bob, who measure polarization at freely chosen angles. The EPR camp argued: the results must be decided in advance by hidden variables, and nothing influences a distant lab. Bell (1964) showed any such local theory must obey |S| ≤ 2 for a clever combination of four correlation settings.

What was observed

Quantum mechanics predicts the singlet correlation E = −cos 2(a−b), which reaches |S| = 2√2 ≈ 2.83 at the optimal settings. Aspect's 1982 experiment — and every loophole-hardened experiment since — measured the quantum value. Local realism is not just incomplete; it is false.

Why it matters

Entanglement is now an engineering resource: quantum key distribution, teleportation and quantum computers. Drag the analyzer angles to feel the correlation curve, then run the CHSH protocol and watch the classical bound get broken.

What remains

"The quantum is not small. It is the smallest thing we can measure — and the smallest thing we can measure turned out to be the whole universe."

From a glowing cavity in Berlin to entangled photons in Paris, the thread is one: nature is quantized, probabilistic and entangled. The technology in your pocket — LEDs, transistors, lasers, photodiodes, MRI machines, atomic clocks — is nothing but this physics, industrialized.
