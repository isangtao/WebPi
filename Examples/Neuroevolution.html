Biologically plausible optimization

Learning without backpropagation

Backpropagation is the workhorse of modern machine learning, yet it is arguably the least biologically plausible part of a neural network: it needs a dedicated backward pass, error signals routed through the circuit, and a near-perfect copy of the forward weights. The brain does none of this. This field guide surveys the credible alternatives — derivative-free numerical methods and evolutionary (genetic) algorithms — that optimize networks using nothing but forward passes and fitness.

01 · The problem

Why backprop feels unbiological

Stochastic gradient descent driven by backpropagation is extraordinarily effective, but several of its core assumptions have no known counterpart in cortical tissue. None of these are proof that the brain does something different — but together they form a strong case.

a The symmetry problem

If two neurons start identical — same weights, same incoming signals — backprop computes identical gradients for both. They stay identical forever. The network can never break symmetry and learn different features, unless a random tie-break is injected by hand.

b Error transport

The update to an early weight requires an error value computed at the output to be routed backward, layer by layer, multiplied by intermediate Jacobians. There is no “error bus” in the cortex; signals flow forward, not backward through the same synapses.

c Global coordination

The change at any single synapse depends on the state of every downstream weight. A local plasticity event would need to read out the precise internal state of distant circuits — an implausible information demand at millisecond timescales.

d Timing & duplication

Long-term potentiation acts within seconds of a stimulus, yet backprop needs the full output error, often arriving much later. And standard backprop effectively keeps a second copy of all weights for the backward pass — brains don’t appear to duplicate their connectome.

02 · The landscape

Two families of plausible optimizers

If you remove gradients and error transport from the equation, two families of methods remain. Numerical, derivative-free methods treat the network as a black box and search its parameter space with forward evaluations only. Evolutionary algorithms maintain a population of candidate networks and let selection, recombination, and variation do the rest.

Derivative-free numerical methods

Random search numerical

plausibility

The honest baseline: sample parameters, keep the best ones seen. No structure, no assumptions, and remarkably competitive — on many hyperparameter and architecture problems it beats carefully tuned heuristics. Conceptually it is the simplest “biological” optimizer: exploration plus memory of what worked.

Mechanism
Sample candidates, retain the best-so-far.
Track record
The yardstick every smarter method must beat; a standard for NAS baselines.

(μ+λ) evolution strategies numerical

plausibility

Keep μ parent parameter vectors; each generation, perturb them with Gaussian noise of step size σ to create λ offspring, evaluate all with a single forward pass, and keep the best μ. A simple “1/5 rule” (grow σ if more than a fifth of steps improved) adapts the step size.

Mechanism
Gaussian perturbations + selection + step-size adaptation.
Track record
Policy-space ES scaled to 18M-parameter Atari agents (2017); a staple for continuous control and HPO.

CMA-ES numerical

plausibility

Covariance Matrix Adaptation: the sampling distribution itself evolves — both its step size and its full covariance — learned from the distribution of successful steps. The gold standard for low-to-medium dimensional continuous black-box optimization, and remarkably robust on ill-conditioned landscapes where plain ES stalls.

Mechanism
Adaptive Gaussian sampling with learned covariance.
Track record
Benchmark king for black-box problems up to ~100 dimensions; robot control, simulation, and HPO.

Differential evolution numerical

plausibility

A population-based scheme with an elegant mutation operator: each new candidate is built from differences between other members of the population (xₘ₁ + F·(xₘ₂ − xₘ₃)). The spread of the population carries the search — no external step size to tune, strong global exploration on multimodal landscapes.

Mechanism
Population differences drive mutation; greedy replacement per slot.
Track record
Widely used in engineering design, antenna optimization, and global black-box search.

Population-based training numerical

plausibility

A population of in-progress training runs, each with its own hyperparameters. Periodically, struggling runs copy the hyperparameters — and sometimes the checkpoints — of top performers. Hyperparameters and weights co-adapt in a purely forward-evaluation loop, no gradient with respect to hyperparameters ever computed.

Mechanism
Periodic exploitation of better hyperparameters across a training population.
Track record
Popular for large RL and HPO pipelines; robust where grid search wastes budget.

Evolutionary (population) algorithms

Genetic algorithms evolutionary

plausibility

The classic: represent candidate solutions as “genomes” (real vectors, topologies, programs), evaluate each in the world, then select the fit, recombine their material, and mutate the result. The workhorse for irregular, combinatorial, and mixed spaces where gradients are undefined or meaningless.

Mechanism
Selection + crossover + mutation over a population of candidates.
Track record
Scheduling, engineering design, architecture search, neuroevolution. See the deep dive below.

NEAT evolutionary

plausibility

Neuroevolution of Augmenting Topologies: instead of evolving weights of a fixed network, evolve the network itself. Start minimal, then add connections and nodes over generations. A bookkeeping system of “innovations” lets structurally different genomes recombine without destroying good circuitry. Often lands small, task-specific circuits.

Mechanism
Evolution of weights and topology, with innovation-preserved crossover.
Track record
Robotic control, cellular-automation discovery, and the backbone of many neuroevolution frameworks.

Evolution of plasticity hybrid

plausibility

The most defensible bridge: evolution does not set weights. It sets the coefficients of a local learning rule (e.g., a simple Hebbian update). At test time the network adapts using only local, forward information — exactly the kind of rule a synapse could implement. Evolution shapes the capacity to learn; learning then happens within a lifetime.

Mechanism
Evolve plasticity parameters; express them with local updates at runtime.
Track record
Strong continual-learning results (EvoPlastic, 2021); the current favorite in bio-plausible ML.

03 · Deep dive

Anatomy of a genetic algorithm

Strip away the biology and a GA is a clean, auditable numerical loop. Five ingredients, all of them implementable in a few dozen lines of code.

1PopulationStart with N random candidates — each a full candidate network or solution.
→
2EvaluateRun each candidate forward only and score it with a fitness function.
→
3SelectFit candidates get more chances to reproduce (tournament, rank, or fitness proportion).
→
4RecombineCrossover mixes two parents; mutation perturbs the children randomly.
→
5New generationReplace the population with the children and repeat until budget runs out.

↻ steps 2–5 repeat every generation — no backward pass ever appears.

Representation

The genome can be a flat vector of weights, a graph (NEAT), a hyperparameter vector, or an entire program. The rest of the algorithm barely cares — it only needs to copy, mix, and perturb it.

Fitness

Any scalar: task reward, accuracy, a cost function — whatever the world offers. Crucially it is computed from forward behavior alone. No labels, no loss gradient, no target network.

Selection

Tournament selection (pick k at random, keep the best) is the most common choice: simple, parallel, and tunable via k. Elitism — copying the top few unchanged — preserves progress.

Recombination

Uniform crossover copies each gene from one parent or the other with 50/50 odds. For structured genomes (topologies, programs) crossover respects structure — this is where NEAT’s innovation numbering earns its keep.

Mutation

Small Gaussian noise for real-valued genomes; bit flips or insertions for symbolic ones. Mutation is what keeps the population from collapsing onto a local optimum — the population is the safety net.

core loop — vanilla javascript
// A minimal generational loop — no gradients anywhere.
function evolve(pop, fitness, generations) {
  for (var g = 0; g < generations; g++) {
    pop.sort(function (a, b) { return fitness(b) - fitness(a); });
    var next = pop.slice(0, 4);                 // elitism: keep the best
    while (next.length < pop.length) {
      var a = tournament(pop, 3);               // pick two parents
      var b = tournament(pop, 3);
      var child = crossover(a, b);              // recombine
      mutate(child, 0.4);                       // random variation
      next.push(child);
    }
    pop = next;                                 // new generation
  }
  return pop[0];
}

function tournament(pop, k) {
  var best = pop[Math.floor(Math.random() * pop.length)];
  for (var i = 1; i < k; i++) {
    var c = pop[Math.floor(Math.random() * pop.length)];
    if (fitness(c) > fitness(best)) best = c;
  }
  return best;
}

function crossover(a, b) {
  var child = [];
  for (var i = 0; i < a.x.length; i++)
    child.push(Math.random() < 0.5 ? a.x[i] : b.x[i]);
  return { x: child };
}

function mutate(ind, sigma) {
  for (var i = 0; i < ind.x.length; i++)
    ind.x[i] += sigma * randn();
  return ind;
}
Why this reads as biologically plausible. Every “unit” in the loop experiences only consequence — it is run, scored, kept or discarded. No error signal is routed backward through the circuit, no synapse needs to read another synapse’s weight, and “learning” is just selection acting across generations. It maps cleanly onto the biology: evolution shapes organisms, and each organism lives one forward pass through the world.

06 · Practice

When to use what

Plausibility and performance are separate axes. Backprop remains unmatched in data efficiency when labels are available. But these methods earn their place in specific corners of the landscape.

Where these methods shine

  • Continuous control with parallel rollouts. If you can evaluate thousands of episodes at once, policy-space ES is simple, stable, and often matches RL baselines.
  • Irregular, discrete, or combinatorial spaces. GAs and evolutionary search handle schedules, layouts, and architecture spaces where gradients are undefined.
  • Small, robust continuous problems. CMA-ES is the default tool for black-box tuning up to ~100 dimensions.
  • Structure matters. NEAT-style methods discover compact, task-specific topologies instead of training a fixed, over-provisioned network.
  • Biologically defensible models. Evolve local plasticity rules and express them at runtime — the closest current bridge between evolution and in-lifetime learning.

Honest limitations

  • Sample complexity. Every evaluation costs a full forward pass (or a full life). Gradients extract far more signal per pass — population methods burn evaluations.
  • Temporal credit assignment. Scoring a whole trajectory gives the circuit no clue which moments mattered. This is still an open problem, even for the hybrids.
  • Scaling. Plain ES degrades in very high dimensions; CMA-ES tops out around a hundred. Large-scale neuroevolution needs parallelism or population tricks like PBT.
  • Local optima are common. Single-line ES walks; populations hedge — but in rugged 1000-dim landscapes, even hedges can fail.
  • Benchmark honestly. Always compare against random search and against the best gradient method for the task before claiming a win.
The most defensible biological story is a division of labor. Slow, generational evolution selects circuits and tunes the coefficients of local plasticity rules; then, within a lifetime, the organism learns with cheap, local, forward-only updates. It is the combination — evolution of learning, rather than learning by evolution — that best reconciles machine-learning evidence with how brains appear to work.
